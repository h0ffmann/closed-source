# Books

Three books are behind this repository, and a fourth is the economic case beside the third. They
are background reading. Since 18 October 2026 the paper cites the first one, *Zen and the Art of
Motorcycle Maintenance* (`pirsig1974zen` in `refs.bib`); it does not cite the others. If a later
version of the paper uses one of them, add the entry to `refs.bib` and log it in
`papers/closed-source/REFERENCES.md`, as AGENTS.md says.

Each entry below has its bibliographic data, where to read it, and why it matters here.

## Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance

> Pirsig, Robert M. *Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance: An Inquiry into Values*. New York:
> William Morrow, 1974. 418 pp. ISBN 0-688-00230-7.

A father and his son go across the United States on a motorcycle. Pirsig uses the journey to ask
what **Quality** is. He makes a difference between "classical" understanding (how a thing works)
and "romantic" understanding (how it looks and feels), and he shows a rider who maintains his own
machine and a rider who must call a mechanic.

**Why it matters here.** Paper Fabric is the maintain-it-yourself rider. The paper, its build and
its website are open, so a reader can take each part apart and put it back together: `just build`,
`just check`, `just site-build`. Closed source is the rider who cannot open the engine. The care
in the build (the reference check, the STE check, the pinned toolchain) is Pirsig's Quality,
applied to a paper.

- Overview: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zen_and_the_Art_of_Motorcycle_Maintenance)

## Democracy: The God That Failed

> Hoppe, Hans-Hermann. *Democracy—The God That Failed: The Economics and Politics of Monarchy,
> Democracy, and Natural Order*. New Brunswick, NJ: Transaction Publishers, 2001. 304 pp.
> ISBN 0-7658-0088-8 (hardcover), 0-7658-0868-4 (paperback). Reissued by Routledge, 2017.

Hoppe compares monarchy and democracy as forms of ownership of a government. His argument is
that a democratic government is a temporary caretaker of the state, so it has a short time
horizon, and that a private-property order ("natural order") is better than both. The title
borrows the formula of *The God That Failed* (1949), the essays by former communists.

**Why it matters here.** The paper's title, *Closed Source: The God that Failed*, uses the same
formula: a system that people believed in, examined, and found to fail. The website's jacket (the
title block in its header) is set after the typography of Hoppe's title. Hoppe also holds that
ideas cannot be property, the position that Kinsella develops below.

**Rights.** Hoppe's argument against intellectual property is a position on what the law should
be. It does not remove the copyright that the law gives now: the book is © 2001, and Hoppe's own
site calls a free online copy of it "unauthorized". So this repository and its website take the
title formula and a typographic style from the book, credit it, and copy no cover art and no
text from it.

- DOI of the Routledge edition: [10.4324/9780203793572](https://doi.org/10.4324/9780203793572) (Crossref, read on 13 October 2026). This repository's Zenodo record names it as a work it `references`.
- Author's page: [hanshoppe.com/democracy](https://www.hanshoppe.com/democracy/)
- Overview: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democracy:_The_God_That_Failed)

## Against Intellectual Property

> Kinsella, Stephan. "Against Intellectual Property." *Journal of Libertarian Studies* 15, no. 2
> (Spring 2001): 1–53.
>
> Book edition: Kinsella, Stephan. *Against Intellectual Property*. Auburn, AL: Ludwig von Mises
> Institute, 2008. 70 pp. ISBN 978-1-933550-32-9.

This is the best-known libertarian case against intellectual property; that it is "the most
famous" is our judgement, not a measured fact. Kinsella, a patent attorney, argues that ideas are
not scarce, because one person's use of an idea does not stop another person's use. Property
rights solve conflicts over scarce things, so patents and copyright are not property rights; they
are state grants that limit what owners of real, scarce things can do with them.

**Why it matters here.** The paper argues that closed source fails in practice. Kinsella argues
that the legal basis of it, the right to stop others copying, fails in principle. The two
arguments are independent: a reader can accept the paper without accepting Kinsella.

- Free text (the Mises Institute publishes it at no cost): [mises.org](https://mises.org/library/book/against-intellectual-property)

## Against Intellectual Monopoly

> Boldrin, Michele, and David K. Levine. *Against Intellectual Monopoly*. Cambridge: Cambridge
> University Press, 2008. 299 pp. ISBN 978-0-521-87928-6.
> DOI [10.1017/CBO9780511510854](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511510854).

Two economists examine the evidence on patents and copyright. They find that innovation and
creative work continue, and often do better, without the monopoly that these laws give. They
discuss software, music, pharmaceuticals and the industrial revolution.

**Why it matters here.** Kinsella's case is about rights; Boldrin and Levine's case is about
results. It is the empirical companion for a reader who does not accept a rights-based argument,
and the closest of the four books to the paper's own method: look at what happens, then decide.

- Publisher: [Cambridge Core](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511510854)
