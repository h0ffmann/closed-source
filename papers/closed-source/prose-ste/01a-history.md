# A short history of the faith {#sec:history}

Closed source was not always the default, and it was never the only view. Three documents, from
1976, 1983 and 1998, show how the faith started, how people argued against it, and how a company
that lived by it left it for the first time. I quote each document at length, because the
arguments of 2026 are already in them.

## 1976: the letter that started the business

On 3 February 1976, a young Bill Gates wrote to the hobbyists who copied his BASIC interpreter for
the Altair computer. He wrote this interpreter with Paul Allen. The letter was in the newsletter of
the Homebrew Computer Club. Its main charge is direct: "As the majority of hobbyists must be
aware, most of you steal your software" [@gates1976letter]. Its main argument is about cost: "The
value of the computer time we have used exceeds $40,000", and "Who can afford to do professional
work for nothing?" [@gates1976letter].

**[ANALYSIS]** The letter is fair on its own terms. In 1976, software was expensive to write,
cheap to copy and difficult to understand from the binary. Secrecy of the source and licences on
the binary were the obvious way to get the cost back. Each premise of that argument changed after
that time. It is now cheaper to write code, because agents write much of it. It was always cheap to copy
code. The third premise was that it is difficult to understand a binary. Section
[-@sec:espionage] shows that this premise is no longer true.

**[ANALYSIS]** The question of the letter is still good: who pays for professional work? Its
answer, secrecy, depends on a cost that is now gone.

## 1983: the announcement that argued against it

On 27 September 1983, Richard Stallman announced on Usenet that he will write a full system that
is compatible with Unix, and give it away free. The name of the system is GNU. His reason was
ethical, not economic: "I consider that the golden rule requires that if I like a program I must
share it with other people who like it" [@stallman1983gnu]. The GNU General Public License came
from this project. Linux uses this licence since 1992 (Section [-@sec:bazaar]).

**[ANALYSIS]** The argument of Stallman and the argument of this paper are related, but they are
not the same. His argument is about the freedom of users. My argument is about how others can test
science, and how authors get credit. Both arguments come to the same practice: publish the source.
Both arguments also have the same enemy. This enemy is not commerce. It is a method that nobody
else can read.

## 1998: a company leaves the faith

On 22 January 1998, Netscape announced that it will make the source code of its next browser
available "for free licensing on the Internet". At that time, Netscape was a leading browser
company. The press release gives the reason: to "harness the creative power of thousands of
programmers on the Internet" [@netscape1998source]. Eric Raymond's essay on the bazaar was
published in the same year [@raymond1998cathedral].

**[ANALYSIS]** Netscape is an early, clear example of a company that decided this: its code had
more value when people read it than when it stayed hidden. The open-weight releases of 2025
(Section [-@sec:openweights]) make the same decision at the scale of AI models. In both examples,
competition caused the change, not charity. In both examples, the company kept other things closed.
Companies do not become generous. But at some point, the value of being read becomes larger than
the value of being hidden, and companies see this.

## What the history shows

**[ANALYSIS]** Fifty years separate the open letter from the REA explosion. In that time, the faith
in closed source started on a real cost. People argued against it for ethical reasons. Companies
calculated again and left it, one part at a time. The evidence from 2024 to 2026 does not start the
story. It removes the last premise of the faith: that nobody reads what is compiled or served.
