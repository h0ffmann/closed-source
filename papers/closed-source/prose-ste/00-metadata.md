abstract: |
  For thirty years, closed source promised that secrecy protects value. We argue that this promise
  failed, and that it failed for science first. Three trends from 2024 to 2026 support this
  argument. Large language models now decompile binaries into re-executable source code. Black-box
  queries extract parts of production models. Distillation copies capabilities at industrial scale.

  Apple's lawsuit against OpenAI is the largest 2026 trade-secret dispute in artificial
  intelligence. It is about people, laptops and recruitment, not about code that stayed secret.
  With this background, we examine Linus Torvalds' Linux and Git again as evidence that openness
  works at a large scale. We also examine the closed undergraduate thesis of one author as evidence
  of the cost of secrecy to a researcher.

  We review obfuscation, which includes an example by one author, and we show why obfuscation
  cannot hide code. We compare it with the idea of John Nash in 1955: security that comes from the
  cost of computation. Signed commits, keys in hardware and two-factor authentication protect what
  secrecy cannot protect.

  Then we argue that professional citation is the ethical answer to universal reverse engineering.
  It has these parts: persistent identifiers, machine-readable citation metadata, and agents that
  borrow, cite and propagate credit by default. We describe Paper Fabric, an open pipeline in the
  Ouroboric style that improves itself and that produced this preprint. We also list its own known
  limitations.
