abstract: |
  For thirty years, closed source promised that secrecy protects value. I argue that this promise
  failed, and that it failed for science first. I examine the case with the book that this book
  stands on: Robert M. Pirsig's *Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance* (1974). Pirsig wrote
  that the number of rational hypotheses that can explain a phenomenon is infinite. Thus science
  does not end, and only the owner of a closed method can test it. Three trends from 2024 to 2026 support this
  argument. Large language models now decompile binaries into re-executable source code. Black-box
  queries extract parts of production models. Distillation copies capabilities at industrial scale.

  Apple's lawsuit against OpenAI is the largest 2026 trade-secret dispute in artificial
  intelligence. It is about people, laptops and recruitment, not about code that stayed secret.
  With this background, I examine Linus Torvalds' Linux and Git again as evidence that openness
  works at a large scale. I also examine my closed undergraduate thesis as evidence
  of the cost of secrecy to a researcher.

  I review obfuscation, which includes an example of my own, and I show why obfuscation
  cannot hide code. I compare it with the idea of John Nash in 1955: security that comes from the
  cost of computation. Signed commits, keys in hardware and two-factor authentication protect what
  secrecy cannot protect.

  Then I argue that professional citation is the ethical answer to universal reverse engineering.
  It has these parts: persistent identifiers, machine-readable citation metadata, and agents that
  borrow, cite and propagate credit by default. I describe Paper Fabric, an open pipeline in the
  Ouroboric style that improves itself and that produced this preprint. I also list its own known
  limitations.
