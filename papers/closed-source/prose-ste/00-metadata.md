abstract: |
  For thirty years, closed source promised that secrecy protects value. I argue that this promise
  failed, and that it failed for science first. I examine the case with the book that this book
  stands on: Robert M. Pirsig's *Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance* (1974). Pirsig wrote
  that the number of rational hypotheses that can explain a phenomenon is infinite. Thus science
  does not end, and only the owner of a closed method can test it. Evidence from 2024 to 12
  November 2026 supports this argument. Large language models now decompile binaries into
  re-executable source code. An open tool, REA (Reverse Engineer Anything), puts the full chain
  behind one agent. After a few weeks, clean-room rebuilds of professional applications were
  available. Black-box queries extract parts of production models. Three US agencies said that
  distillation copies capabilities at industrial scale.

  Secrets also leak from inside. Apple's lawsuit against OpenAI is the largest 2026 trade-secret
  dispute in artificial intelligence. It is about people, laptops and recruitment, not about code
  that stayed secret. A jury convicted a former Google engineer who took AI secrets through a
  personal cloud account. A packaging error published the source of a commercial agent. At the
  same time, the closed laboratories started to publish weights and methods. I examine Linus
  Torvalds' Linux and Git again as evidence that openness works at a large scale. I examine
  Heartbleed and the xz backdoor as evidence that people can find open failures. I read the law of
  the United States, the European Union and Brazil. All three accept reverse engineering of a
  product that you hold lawfully.

  I review obfuscation, which includes an example of my own, and I show why obfuscation
  cannot hide code. I compare it with the idea of John Nash in 1955: security that comes from the
  cost of computation. Signed commits, keys in hardware and two-factor authentication protect what
  secrecy cannot protect.

  Then I argue that professional citation is the ethical answer to universal reverse engineering.
  It has these parts: persistent identifiers, machine-readable citation metadata, and agents that
  borrow, cite and propagate credit by default. I describe Paper Fabric, an open pipeline
  that improves itself and that produced this preprint. I answer six objections,
  and I list the known limitations of the paper.
