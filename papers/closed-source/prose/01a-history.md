# A short history of the faith {#sec:history}

Closed source was not always the default, and it was never the only view. Three documents, from
1976, 1983 and 1998, mark how the faith was founded, contested and first abandoned by a company
that had lived by it. I quote each at length because the arguments of 2026 are already in them.

## 1976: the letter that founded the business

On 3 February 1976 a young Bill Gates wrote to the hobbyists who copied the BASIC interpreter he
had written with Paul Allen for the Altair computer. The letter appeared in the newsletter of the
Homebrew Computer Club. Its central charge is blunt: "As the majority of hobbyists must be aware,
most of you steal your software" [@gates1976letter]. Its central argument is about cost: "The
value of the computer time we have used exceeds $40,000", and "Who can afford to do professional
work for nothing?" [@gates1976letter].

**[ANALYSIS]** The letter is fair on its own terms. In 1976 software was expensive to write, cheap
to copy and hard to understand from the binary. Secrecy of the source and licences on the binary
were the obvious way to make the cost back. Every premise of that argument has since moved. Writing
code is cheaper, since agents write much of it; copying was always cheap; and the third premise,
that a binary is hard to understand, is the one that Section [-@sec:espionage] shows has collapsed.
The letter's question, who pays for professional work, remains good. Its answer, secrecy, rests on
a cost that no longer exists.

## 1983: the announcement that contested it

On 27 September 1983 Richard Stallman announced on Usenet that he would write a complete
Unix-compatible system and give it away: GNU. His reason was ethical, not economic: "I consider
that the golden rule requires that if I like a program I must share it with other people who like
it" [@stallman1983gnu]. The GNU General Public License that came out of the project is the licence
under which Linux was released from 1992 (Section [-@sec:bazaar]).

**[ANALYSIS]** Stallman's argument and this paper's are related but not the same. His is an
argument about the freedom of users; mine is an argument about the testability of science and the
credit of authors. They meet at the same practice: publish the source. They also meet at the same
enemy, which is not commerce but a method that nobody else can read.

## 1998: a company leaves the faith

On 22 January 1998 Netscape, then a leading browser company, announced that it would make the
source code of its next browser available "for free licensing on the Internet". The press release
gives the reason: to "harness the creative power of thousands of programmers on the Internet"
[@netscape1998source]. Eric Raymond's essay on the bazaar was published the same year
[@raymond1998cathedral].

**[ANALYSIS]** Netscape is an early, clear case of a firm that concluded that its code was worth
more read than hidden. The open-weight releases of 2025 (Section [-@sec:openweights]) repeat the
decision at the scale of AI models. In both cases the move came under competitive pressure, not
out of charity, and in both cases the company kept other things closed. The pattern is not that
companies become generous. It is that the value of being read, at some point, exceeds the value of
being hidden, and companies notice.

## What the history shows

**[ANALYSIS]** Fifty years separate the open letter from the REA explosion. In that time the faith
in closed source was founded on a real cost, contested on ethical grounds, and abandoned piece by
piece by firms that did the arithmetic again. The 2024 to 2026 evidence does not start the story.
It removes the last premise on which the faith rested, that what is compiled or served stays
unread.
