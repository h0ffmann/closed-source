# The Man Who Warned (Edmund Burke)

Featured in [the map of content](index.md). Every fact here has its source and page in
[[202610130526-burke-and-the-watt-patent-1775]]; this note is the reading of those facts.

## The warning

In 1775 Boulton and Watt asked Parliament to extend the 1769 patent on the steam engine. The
mining interest answered with the cry of "No monopoly" (Smiles, p. 210). Edmund Burke, member
for Bristol, stood with it: "in 1775, Burke raised his voice for freedom, and against the
patent" (Lord, p. 101). He lost. The Act passed, and the patent ran to 1800.

Later writers named Burke the founder of conservatism, a word he did not use of himself. So the
man whom tradition gives to the defenders of old institutions is, in this one case, on record
against a longer monopoly on a machine.

## What he did in the same year

In the same session Burke backed the bill of Richard Champion, of Bristol, to extend
Cookworthy's porcelain patent, against Josiah Wedgwood, who called that extension "a Monopoly
injurious to the Community at large" (Owen, p. 121). The bill passed the Commons by a majority
of 49, and Champion wrote: "it is to him I am indebted for all" (Owen, pp. 114, 143). Lord, who
is the source of the famous sentence about Burke and Watt, wrote of the two bills: "the cases
seem exactly parallel" (p. 142).

So the warning was not a doctrine. No source that I read gives Burke's words against Watt's
bill. The motive is a guess in every account: the mines near Bristol (Smiles), a hatred of
monopolies and a quarrel with Wedderburn (Lord). A study of the contemporary evidence concludes
that Burke was "very much in favour of granting monopolies to inventors as well as authors"
(Johnson, 2016, abstract).

## What the warning is worth

**[ANALYSIS]** The owner's title holds in a narrow sense, and the narrow sense is the useful
one. Burke did not warn against patents. He opposed one extension: a term already given, to be
made longer, for an invention already made. His two stands of 1775 show how such questions were
decided: by whose constituents won and whose lost. That is the failure that
[[202610130528-patents-the-god-that-failed]] and
[[202610130527-authors-rights-the-god-that-failed]] describe: the price of the monopoly is set by
those who hold it and their friends in Parliament, and the public that pays is not in the room.

**[ANALYSIS]** The second lesson is about citation, which is this book's subject. Boldrin and
Levine's sentence, that Burke "spoke eloquently in Parliament in the name of economic freedom",
is the form in which most readers meet this story. Its source is Lord, and Lord's next mention
of Burke after that passage is the Champion bill. The sentence travelled, and the complication
did not. A reader who follows the citation finds both. An agent that repeats the sentence
without the source finds neither.
