# Open source first for science, after Pirsig

My position: open source should come first for science, and the best reference of this book for
that is Robert M. Pirsig.

**The chain, in four steps.**

1. Pirsig: the number of rational hypotheses for any phenomenon is infinite, so science does
   not close ([[202610181353-pirsig-infinite-hypotheses-science-does-not-close]]).
2. If science does not close, no one laboratory can test all of it. The tests must be shared.
3. A closed method can be tested only by its owner. Every other laboratory must trust it, and
   trust is not a test. So closed science leaves a hypothesis untested by the people who could
   test it ([[202610132127-private-code-and-private-science-lose-their-value]]).
4. In 2026, agents make hypotheses faster than ever, and they copy at scale
   ([[202610151812-what-is-distillation]]). The multitude grows faster; the case for open
   methods grows with it.

**Where it is in the book.** The abstract and the introduction now name Pirsig's *Zen and the
Art of Motorcycle Maintenance* (1974) as the book this one stands on, with sentence 2 of the
literature note (`papers/closed-source/prose/01-introduction.md`). In `.zenodo.json` the book is
a `references` tie by its ISBN, 0688002307; Zenodo shows it from the next release.

**Status: open question.** Steps 2 to 4 are my reading, not Pirsig's: he wrote about one
student and the scientific method, not about source code. What would test it: a case where a
closed result was wrong and only an outside lab could have found it, or the reverse, a closed
lab that tested a hypothesis that nobody outside could have tested.

The essay: [The hypotheses have no end, 2026](../essays/2026-the-hypotheses-have-no-end.md).
