# Burke opposed the extension of Watt's patent, 1775

The owner asked for a note that Edmund Burke, "creator of conservatism philosophy", was against
the efforts of his time to give patents to machines. The sources support a narrower sentence:

> In 1775 Edmund Burke, whom later writers named the founder of conservatism, opposed the Act of
> Parliament that extended James Watt's steam-engine patent to 1800. In the same year he backed
> the bill that extended a porcelain patent for Richard Champion of Bristol, the city he
> represented.

## What the sources say

- **The Act.** Watt's patent dates from 1769. In 1775 an Act of Parliament extended it to 1800
  (Lord, p. 101; Boldrin and Levine, ch. 1).
- **Burke opposed it.** Smiles, p. 210, on the supporters of the mining interest in Parliament:
  "among the most strenuous we find the name of Edmund Burke". Lord, p. 101: "in 1775, Burke
  raised his voice for freedom, and against the patent". Boldrin and Levine, ch. 1: "The great
  statesman Edmund Burke spoke eloquently in Parliament in the name of economic freedom and
  against the creation of unnecessary monopoly – but to no avail." Their note 1 gives Lord as the
  source of this sentence.
- **Why he opposed it is a guess in every source.** Smiles, p. 210: he was "influenced, it is
  supposed, by certain mining interests in the neighbourhood of Bristol, which city he then
  represented". Lord, p. 102, doubts that: "Burke's opposition was more probably due to his
  natural hatred of monopolies, and to the fact that Boulton and Watt had applied for their Act
  on the advice of Wedderburn, with whom Burke at this time was struggling over the American
  question in the Commons."
- **The label.** The *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* ("Conservatism", section 1) reports
  that Cecil's *Conservatism* (1912) has a chapter on "Burke as the founder of conservatism", and
  that the date of this adoption is disputed. Section 2.1: Burke's French disciples coined the
  word "conservative"; "Burke himself used 'conserve'". Burke did not call himself a
  conservative.

## What complicates this

**In the same session Burke took the other side on another patent.** Each statement below has
its source.

1. On 22 February 1775 Richard Champion petitioned the Commons for a bill "to enlarge the term
   of the letters patent granted to William Cookworthy" for porcelain. The petition went to a
   committee that named "Mr. Burke" first (Owen, p. 115, from the Commons Journals).
2. Josiah Wedgwood opposed the bill for the Staffordshire potters. His memorial called the
   extension "a Monopoly injurious to the Community at large" (Owen, p. 121).
3. Burke backed the bill. Owen, p. 114, writes of the "cordial support of Edmund Burke, and of
   the whig members generally"; the bill "passed the Commons by a majority of 49, without
   amendment". Champion wrote afterwards: "I do not say how much I am indebted to Mr. Burke for
   the part he took in this affair, for it is to him I am indebted for all" (Owen, p. 143). The
   Lords then added a clause that, in Owen's judgement, took away the advantage (p. 114).
4. Lord, the source behind Boldrin and Levine's sentence, sees the conflict himself (p. 142):
   "It is curious to find Burke on two different sides with regard to this patent extension and
   that of Watt: the cases seem exactly parallel." Boldrin and Levine do not repeat this.
5. Wedgwood's memorial says that Watt's extension had "been urged in favour of Mr. Champion's
   Application", and separates the cases: Watt was "the original Inventor of the Machine",
   Champion "the purchaser only" of another man's patent (Owen, p. 121).
6. Johnson (2016) studies this question. Its abstract says that Burke's reputation as an
   opponent of intellectual property "arises largely through his opposition to James Watt's Fire
   Engine Act 1775", and that the contemporary evidence shows "that Burke's views were actually
   very much in favour of granting monopolies to inventors as well as authors".

**[ANALYSIS]** Three things follow. First, "Burke was against patents on machines" says more than
the record does: the record has one opposition, to one extension of one patent, with no text of
a speech in the sources I read. Second, Bristol is on both sides of 1775. The mining interest
near Bristol wanted Watt's engine free (Smiles), and a Bristol manufacturer wanted his own patent
longer (Owen). So his vote against Watt may show the interests of his city as much as a
principle, and no source here decides between them. Third, the two cases differ in one way that
keeps the owner's word "machines" partly true: Burke opposed the patent on a machine and backed
the patent on a material and its process. No source I read says that Burke made that
distinction.

## Not verified

- **No speech.** I found no report of what Burke said against Watt's bill. Not read: the Commons
  Journals for 1775, Cobbett's *Parliamentary History*, Burke's *Correspondence*, and E.
  Robinson, "Matthew Boulton and the Art of Parliamentary Lobbying", *The Historical Journal* 7
  (1964). "Spoke eloquently" (Boldrin and Levine) rests on Lord's "raised his voice".
- **Johnson (2016), full text.** The repository copies (Cardiff ORCA, CORE) and the publisher
  refused the download on 2026-10-13. I read the abstract only, from Crossref. It is the first
  source to read next.
- **Dates.** Smiles, p. 210, says Watt's bill was introduced on 28 February 1775. Lord, p. 101,
  says it was "from February 22nd till May 8th in passing". The two do not agree, and I did not
  check either against the Journals.
- **Quotations from Smiles, Lord and Owen** come from the Internet Archive's scans, read as
  machine-read text. I quote only passages that the text gives without damage.
